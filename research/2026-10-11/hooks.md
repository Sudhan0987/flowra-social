# Hook library (2026-10-11)

Studied 14 top Shorts; saw the real opening (frames and words) of 0, the first frame and cover of the rest.

This week's set has 14 Shorts. Eleven are from last week, three are new (caAQu4gisjQ, RCbQYtV0TIw, qmSDBQ8BI_w), and we again have only first frames and covers, with no spoken words. The openings that hold put the problem, a reaction or a promise in frame one, using an animated character in shock, a caption with a count, or a judged report. The weak ones start on a bare screen recording, a greeting or a mascot that only appears on the cover. Our own first saves and shares came from r006, which opens on good news with a catch (Google's AI shows your page while your clicks fall), so that structure stays in the library on our own evidence.

## 1. The animated action
Formula: [Character] panics at [the problem you have], then does [one physical action] that fixes it.

The first frame is a character in shock with the problem in front of him, so the viewer feels the trouble before any words. The second beat is one big, satisfying motion that releases the tension and shows this is animation, not someone reading an ad. It is the format closest to Floey's. Picture: Frame one: an animated character with wide eyes over scattered charts. Then a cut to the same character calmly slamming a glowing button. For Floey: she freezes, wide-eyed, over a wilting flower, then dives in and trims the one petal that slipped.

## 2. The flat contradiction
Formula: [The thing your field swears by] doesn't [do what you think]. [The overlooked thing] does.

A short, confident claim against what the viewer believes creates an itch only the explanation can scratch, and it takes under two seconds to say. Both examples come from real channels with 8,000 and 23,300 subscribers, so this is some of the steadiest evidence in the set. It only works when the claim is on screen or spoken right away, not left to the title. Picture: A character caught mid-expression in frame one, with the challenged word appearing around them within a second and a half. For Floey: she flies past a shiny new flower to a drooping old one while the word 'new' fades out.

## 4. The numbered promise
Formula: [Get the result you want] in [a real, small number of] steps: [step], [step], [step].

## 6. Side by side
Formula: Your [usual view or approach] next to [what it hides], with the second one appearing beside the first.

Showing one thing and then putting a second beside it makes the eye compare, and the gap between them is the hook before anyone speaks. One example opens on a single AI Overview above a reaction face, then the conflicting result appears beside it. The other has two counters on screen from frame one. Picture: One panel in frame one, the second sliding in beside it by 1.5 seconds, with a face or character reacting below. For Floey: one flower labelled 'before', then 'after' grows beside it. Show no figures unless they are a real measured result.

## Avoid
- Greetings and self-introductions ('Hi, I'm Floey'). Both SpeedyIndex Shorts open on a 'Hi!' caption and score among the lowest in the set, even though they come from one of the biggest channels. Greetings also break the house rules and waste the 2 seconds a hook has to land.
- A mascot that only appears on the cover. The new otter Short (qmSDBQ8BI_w) has a charming animated character on its cover but opens on an empty tool screen, and it has the lowest score in the set. Floey has to be in frame one, reacting to the problem.
- Putting the hook only on the cover. In the Shorts feed the video autoplays from frame one and the cover is never seen. Several videos open on a raw Pinterest recording, an empty search box or a person standing silently, and leave the hook to the cover.
- A slow start before the problem (a logo, an establishing shot of the garden, Floey flying in). Instagram viewers still leave our reels within about 2 to 4 seconds, so the problem has to be on screen and spoken in frame one.
- Unedited screen recordings as the opening. A plain browser, analytics or tool screen with no caption, face or highlight gives the viewer nothing to react to. If we show a chart, Floey reacts to it and one line is highlighted.
- Talking heads with no caption in frame one. The new Turkish hospital Short opens on a person walking with no text, so anyone watching muted gets no hook. Always pair Floey's line with one on-screen caption.
- Assuming one platform suits one kind of opening. Earlier, news reels seemed to do better on YouTube, but r006, a news reel, has now jumped on Instagram. Keep testing problem-led and news-led openings on both platforms.
- Yes or no questions copied from strangers' titles ('Can your website handle the traffic?'). The viewer can answer 'yes' and scroll away. Restate them as a specific claim about the viewer's site.
- Hype numbers and invented counters ('Drive millions', 'Rank and scale faster', '50% not on page 1', made-up before and after figures). A step count is fine only if Flowra's process really has that many steps.
- Indexing promises like the SpeedyIndex reels make. Flowra asks Google to recrawl on publish and monitors index coverage, but it never guarantees indexing.
- Walls of text and stat collages (several badges, 'FULL GUIDE', the 'Intel Report' collage). Nobody can read that much in the first seconds. Use one line, at most two.
- Long tutorial setups (the 109 to 179 second videos). They promise step-by-step lessons, which doesn't suit Flowra's 25 to 45 second reels. Open on the single loss, not the course outline.
- Treating the ranking as exact. There are still no spoken openings, and the top scores come from channels with 0 to 99 subscribers, which inflates views per subscriber. The steadiest evidence is from the 8,000 to 241,000 subscriber channels and from our own r006.
